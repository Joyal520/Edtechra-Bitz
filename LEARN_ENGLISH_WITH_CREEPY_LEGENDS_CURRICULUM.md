## SECTION 3 — SIMPLE ENGLISH STORY
Overtoun Bridge is a beautiful stone bridge in Dumbarton, Scotland. It was built in 1895 near a large mansion called Overtoun House. The bridge crosses a deep river valley with tall green trees and rocky ground 50 feet below.

For many years, local dog owners noticed something very strange. According to the story, dozens of pet dogs ran across the bridge and suddenly jumped over the stone parapet wall. Most of the jumps happened on clear, sunny days. Even more strangely, almost all the dogs jumped from the exact same spot on the right side of the bridge.

Local people were worried and confused. Some people believed in supernatural stories. A local legend says that the ghost of the "White Lady of Overtoun" walks near the bridge and causes the animals to leap into the air.

However, scientists and animal experts wanted to find a natural explanation. An expert named Dr. David Sands studied the bridge. He discovered that wild minks, mice, and squirrels lived in the bushes below the bridge. Minks have a very strong, sweet smell. Dogs have a powerful sense of smell.

Experts believe that when dogs walked across the bridge, they smelled the minks below. Because the stone walls of the bridge are high and thick, the dogs could not see how far the drop was. They jumped over the wall to catch the animal, not knowing there was a 50-foot fall.

Today, warning signs on the bridge tell owners: "Please keep your dog on a leash." Whether you believe in ghosts or wild animal smells, Overtoun Bridge remains one of Scotland's most famous mysteries.

## SECTION 5 — GRAMMAR
### Past Simple of 'To Be' (*Was / Were*)

We use **was** and **were** to describe people, places, and situations in the past.

* Use **was** with singular subjects: **I, He, She, It**
* Use **were** with plural subjects: **You, We, They**

#### Examples:
1. The stone bridge **was** built in 1895.
2. The weather **was** clear and sunny.
3. The dogs **were** curious about the animal smell.
4. Many pet owners **were** worried and confused.

According to an old urban legend, a man was driving home late at night. The weather was cold and rainy. The country road was dark and empty.

Suddenly, his car headlights showed a young woman standing by the side of the road. She wore a simple white dress and carried no bag or umbrella. The driver felt sorry for her, so he stopped his car.

## SECTION 5 — GRAMMAR
### Past Simple Regular Verbs (*-ed*)

To talk about finished actions in the past with regular verbs, we add **-ed** (or **-d** if the verb ends in *e*).

* *walk* $\rightarrow$ **walked**
* *stop* $\rightarrow$ **stopped**
* *knock* $\rightarrow$ **knocked**
* *arrive* $\rightarrow$ **arrived**

#### Examples:
1. The driver **stopped** his car in the rain.
2. The young woman **opened** the car door.
3. The man **walked** to the front door and **knocked**.
4. The car **arrived** at the old house after twenty minutes.

## SECTION 3 — SIMPLE ENGLISH STORY
According to Japanese urban folklore, Kuchisake-onna is a mysterious woman who walks through quiet streets at dusk or late in the evening. She wears a long trench coat and a surgical mask over her mouth.

In Japan, wearing a mask when you have a cold is very common, so at first, nobody suspects anything strange. But the legend says that if she meets a child or traveler walking alone, she stops and asks a simple question: "Am I pretty?"

If the traveler says "No," she becomes angry. If the traveler says "Yes," she removes her mask. Behind the mask, her mouth is slit wide from ear to ear into a frightening smile. Then she asks again: "Even now?"

The legend says you cannot run away easily because she is extraordinarily fast. However, clever students in Japan shared tricks to escape safely!

According to the story, if you give a confusing answer like "So-so" or "You look average," she pauses to think about what you mean. This gives you time to run away. Another story says that if you throw hard candies or fruit, she stops to pick them up, and you can escape.

Folklorists explain that Kuchisake-onna became widely popular in the late 1970s. Rumors spread quickly through schools across Japan. While it is purely an urban myth, it taught children an important real-life safety rule: walk home in groups and do not talk to strangers on dark streets.

## SECTION 5 — GRAMMAR
### Question Words (*Who, What, Where, Why, How*)

We use question words at the start of a question to ask for specific information:

* **Who** asks about a person $\rightarrow$ *Who is the woman?*
* **What** asks about a thing or action $\rightarrow$ *What does she wear?*
* **Where** asks about a place $\rightarrow$ *Where does she walk?*
* **Why** asks for a reason $\rightarrow$ *Why do students throw candy?*
* **How** asks about manner or method $\rightarrow$ *How can you escape?*

#### Examples:
1. **Who** is Kuchisake-onna? (She is a legendary woman.)
2. **What** does she wear over her face? (She wears a mask.)
3. **Where** does she appear? (On quiet streets in Japan.)
4. **Why** do people say "So-so"? (To confuse her and escape.)

## SECTION 3 — SIMPLE ENGLISH STORY
In Room 62 of the world-famous British Museum in London, there is an ancient Egyptian coffin lid known as Object 22542. It belonged to a priestess of Amen-Ra who lived more than 3,000 years ago.

In the late 19th century, an English traveler bought the painted wooden lid in Egypt. Soon after, strange stories began to spread. According to the legend, bad luck followed anyone who owned or touched the artifact. People whispered that the buyer lost his arm in an accident, and other owners experienced sudden illness or lost their money.

Eventually, the owners gave the artifact to the British Museum. But the creepy rumors did not stop! Journalists wrote dramatic articles claiming that guards heard crying and banging inside the glass display case at night.

The most famous rumor began in 1912. A sensational story claimed that an American millionaire bought the "Unlucky Mummy" and put it secretly on board the Titanic to transport it to New York. When the Titanic struck an iceberg and sank into the cold Atlantic Ocean, believers cried: "The mummy cursed the ship!"

However, historians and museum records tell a very different truth. The mummy board never left London. In fact, official records show that Object 22542 stayed safely inside the British Museum throughout 1912, and you can still see it there today.

The "curse" was actually invented by creative journalists and storytellers who loved a thrilling mystery. It shows how easily an urban legend can become attached to a real historical disaster.

## SECTION 3 — SIMPLE ENGLISH STORY
Bloody Mary is one of the most famous folklore rituals in the English-speaking world. For generations, children and teenagers have tested their bravery by playing this eerie game at sleepovers.

According to the legend, the rules are very specific. You must go into a dark bathroom alone at midnight. Turn off all electric lights and light a single candle. Stand directly in front of the mirror and stare into your reflection. Then, chant the name: "Bloody Mary, Bloody Mary, Bloody Mary."

The story claims that after you say the name three times (or thirteen times in some versions), a ghostly woman will appear in the glass behind you.

Where did this legend come from? Folklorists trace the ritual back to the 19th century. In Victorian times, young women performed mirror games on Halloween night to predict their future husbands. They walked backward up stairs holding a candle and looked in a hand mirror, hoping to see a handsome face.

However, science provides a fascinating explanation for why people see strange faces in dark mirrors today. It is called the *Troxler Effect*, discovered by physician Ignaz Troxler in 1804.

When you stare at a fixed point in dim light for more than a minute, your brain gets tired of the unchanging image. To save energy, your visual system starts fading details and creates hallucinations or distorted shapes. The scary face in the mirror is not a ghost—it is your own brain playing a visual trick!

## SECTION 3 — SIMPLE ENGLISH STORY
In the 12th century, during the reign of King Stephen of England, a strange event was recorded by two respected medieval chroniclers, Ralph of Coggeshall and William of Newburgh.

Near the small village of Woolpit in Suffolk, harvesters were working in the fields. Suddenly, they discovered two young children—a boy and his sister—standing near old wolf pits.

The children were completely unusual. Their skin was tinted bright green. They wore strange clothes made of unfamiliar fabrics, and they spoke a language that no one in the village could understand.

The villagers took the frightened children to the manor house of Sir Richard de Calne. The children were crying and hungry, but they refused to eat bread, meat, or fruit. For days they ate nothing, until villagers brought raw green bean stalks into the kitchen. The children opened the stalks eagerly and survived on the beans.

Over time, the boy grew weak and passed away, but the girl survived and adapted to English life. Her diet expanded, and gradually her skin lost its green color and became normal.

The girl learned to speak English and was baptized. When people asked where she came from, she described a subterranean homeland called "St. Martin's Land." According to her story, the sun never shone there; everything was twilight, and all the inhabitants were green. She claimed they were watching their father's sheep when they heard church bells, followed the sound into a cave, and emerged into the bright English sunshine.

Modern historians offer a simpler explanation: the children may have been Flemish immigrants who became lost after their parents died. Their green skin might have been caused by malnutrition (chlorosis or green sickness), which disappeared after eating healthy food!

## SECTION 5 — GRAMMAR
### Comparatives and Superlatives (*-er, -est, more, most*)

We use **comparatives** to compare two things, and **superlatives** to compare three or more things:

* **Short Adjectives:**
  * Comparative: add **-er** $\rightarrow$ *green $\rightarrow$ greener, strange $\rightarrow$ stranger, old $\rightarrow$ older*
  * Superlative: add **the ... -est** $\rightarrow$ *the greenest, the strangest, the oldest*
* **Long Adjectives (2+ syllables):**
  * Comparative: use **more** $\rightarrow$ *more mysterious, more unusual, more famous*
  * Superlative: use **the most** $\rightarrow$ *the most mysterious, the most unusual, the most famous*

#### Examples:
1. The children were **stranger than** ordinary travelers.
2. Woolpit is **older than** modern cities.
3. This is **the most mysterious** chronicle of the 12th century.
4. The girl became **healthier** after eating balanced food.
